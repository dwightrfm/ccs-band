Cost

What does band actually cost per month?

It depends on the instrument and how often your student takes lessons. There's a calculator on the family page that gives you a real number — pick the instrument, set the lesson rate and frequency, and it shows the monthly cost and the full-year total. At the low end, most instruments run about $130 a month with weekly lessons, and lessons are the large majority of that. The instrument rental itself is $30 a month for most instruments.

Can we buy an instrument instead of renting?

You can. Joyful Noise applies 50% of what you've already paid in rental fees toward buying a used instrument later, so renting first doesn't waste money if you decide to purchase. Talk to them directly — 919-386-9335. My advice is to rent for the first year. Beginning students change instruments more often than parents expect, and a rental keeps that from being an expensive mistake.

What if we can't afford the rental or lessons?

Choir. It's a full music class with no instrument to rent, no book to buy, and no lesson cost — and students who start in choir often move into band in a later year. Nobody should be sitting out music because of what band costs. If choir isn't the right fit for your student, email me directly and we'll talk it through.

Can my student drop band partway through the year?

No, not by choice. Band runs the full year — the group depends on every part being there, and a section losing a player mid-year affects everyone else in it. The exceptions are a medical reason or special permission from administration. If something is going on that makes band feel impossible, email me before it gets to that point; most of the time there's a fix that doesn't involve leaving.

Should we keep the rental over the summer?

That's your call and you arrange it directly with Joyful Noise — they send a return-and-summer-rental notice near the end of the year. Keeping it means your student can play over the summer, which makes September much easier. Returning it is fine too. If your student is thinking about switching instruments, tell me before you decide, so the switch and the rental line up.

Equipment

Where do I buy the book, and which one?

Essential Elements for Band, Book 1 — in the edition for your student's specific instrument. It runs about $15 on Amazon or from any music retailer. Get the right instrument's version; a trumpet book won't work for a clarinet. Every student needs their own copy.

My student plays percussion. What do we buy, and how loud is it at home?

The school owns the drums, so there is nothing to rent. Snare sticks are the only requirement — about $13. For home practice, a practice pad with a stand runs about $30, is quiet enough for a bedroom, and is the best value of the options for building technique. A full beginner snare kit is around $69 and is genuinely loud; the mute pad it comes with helps. The pad is what I'd recommend for most families.

What happens if my student's instrument breaks or needs repair?

Contact Joyful Noise directly — repairs and maintenance are included in the rental. They keep a small number of loaner instruments in the band room, so your student can use a loaner and keep playing while theirs is being fixed. Tell me too, so I know why they're on a different instrument that week.

What does a percussion student need to start?

A practice pad and a pair of snare sticks. That's it to begin with — the school owns the drums and the bells, so there's nothing to rent. A pad and sticks is what I tell every new percussion family.

Instrument Choice

My student has braces. Which instruments still work?

Braces don't rule out band. Flute, clarinet, and saxophone are usually the easiest adjustment. Trumpet and trombone press a mouthpiece directly against the lips, so the first couple of weeks after braces go on — or come off — take some getting used to; plenty of students play brass with braces, and orthodontic wax helps a lot. Percussion is unaffected. If your student has braces or is getting them soon, tell me before instrument choices are made and I'll factor it in.

When does band start, and when do 4th graders get their instrument?

Two dates to put on your calendar. **Tuesday, August 11** is the first day of band with instruments — if your student already owns one, bring it that day. **Thursday, August 13** is when instruments are assigned to everyone who needs one. Seats in each section are limited, so not every student gets their first choice; I balance the band so it actually sounds like a band.

We already own an instrument. Can my student use it?

Yes — bring it on Tuesday, August 11, the first day of band with instruments. There's nothing to rent and nothing to arrange. Just tell me ahead of time what your student has so I can plan the section.

My student takes piano. Which instrument should they consider?

Percussion. Students who already read music and have keyboard experience pick up the bells and the reading side of percussion much faster than students starting from zero, and percussion needs exactly that skill set. It's a long-standing recommendation here and usually the best fit for a piano student joining band.

Lessons

Why aren't you teaching private lessons anymore?

I taught private lessons at school through last year and families valued them. For 2026-2027 I'm not teaching lessons, so that time goes into the band classes themselves. Families arrange lessons directly with a private instructor — the Private Teacher List on the family page has local teachers organized by instrument, with phone and email.

Where does the private teacher list come from? Are the teachers screened?

The list is maintained by Music & Arts, the music retailer — these are working professional instructors the store knows and refers, not names pulled off the internet. That said, private lessons are an arrangement between your family and the teacher, not a school program: CCS doesn't employ these instructors and doesn't run background checks on them. Do the same due diligence you would for any private tutor — ask about experience and references, and for a younger student, sit in on the first lesson or two. If a teacher on that list ever gives you a reason for concern, tell me and I'll look into it.

Can you recommend a private teacher for my student's instrument?

Yes — email me and tell me the instrument, and I'll point you to the teachers on the list I'd start with for that instrument. The full Private Teacher List is linked on the Band Family Info page, but it's long, so if you'd rather I narrow it down for you, just ask.

When should we set lessons up?

Early — the first few weeks of the year if you can. Students who start lessons in September are in a completely different place by the Christmas concert than students who start in January. If you've already arranged a teacher, you're set; if not, the Private Teacher List is the place to start.

Our private teacher isn't showing up consistently. What should we do?

Switch. There's a full list of private teachers to choose from linked on the Band Family Info page — pick another one from it. You're not locked in to the first teacher you tried, and a teacher who misses weeks isn't going to get your student where they want to be.

How often should my student take lessons?

Weekly is the recommendation. Local teachers generally run $25 to $40 an hour. Lessons are not required to be in band, but a student taking them weekly progresses noticeably faster than one who doesn't — and the students who stay in band through middle and high school are almost always the ones who had a private teacher early.

Practice

How much should my student practice?

Three times a week, 30 minutes per session, minimum — every grade, no exceptions. Class time alone isn't enough to make progress. This is the single thing that most separates students who thrive in band from students who quit.

How is practice tracked, and what happens if my student falls behind?

Practice is logged in MakeMusic Cloud. If ten or more days pass with nothing logged, you'll get a short email from me — that's a heads-up, not a warning, and there's no classroom penalty attached to it. The practice commitment is between you and your student at home; my job is to make sure it isn't quietly slipping.

Practicing at home is loud. Can my student use a mute?

At home, yes — a mute is worth trying if noise is a problem. Ask Joyful Noise or any music retailer. I don't use them in class though: the band room's acoustics make everything loud regardless, and mutes distort the sound enough to be a distraction while we're rehearsing.

Ukulele

Does my 3rd grader need to buy a ukulele or a book?

Neither. The ukulele is provided, and there's no book to buy — everything students need to learn is in a presentation I use in class. There is nothing to purchase for ukulele. Full details are on the Ukulele page.

Can the ukulele come home?

Yes, on Tuesday and Wednesday nights. Every other night the ukulele stays at school — instruments are shared, and weekends are where they get lost or damaged.

Is ukulele practice tracked like band practice?

No. Students are expected to practice enough to keep up with the class, but it runs on the honor system — no log, no app. A few minutes on the days the ukulele comes home is plenty at this age.

Do ukulele families sign the band participation agreement?

No. That agreement is for band students who rent an instrument through Joyful Noise. Ukulele families have nothing to sign.

Can my 3rd grader take the ukulele home over a school break?

Yes. The one thing I ask is that you email me before the instrument leaves the building for a break, so I know where every ukulele is over a long weekend or holiday.

Communication

How will I know how my student is doing?

I post a band update in Veracross every two weeks — what we're working on and what's coming up. Grades are in Veracross as well. If your student is struggling, you'll hear from me directly; I'm not going to let you find out from a report card. Any email you send gets a reply within 24 hours.

How is band graded?

Lower school band isn't a letter grade — it's an S / U / E mark on the report card. By default a student earns an S if they're putting in the effort and practicing, which is most of the class. An E is for students going clearly beyond that. Alongside the mark there are periodic playing assessments — scale quizzes from the Essential Elements book, and a short snare solo for percussionists — so the grade reflects real playing, not just attendance.

What's the difference between an S and an E on the report card?

An S means your student is doing what band asks — coming prepared, putting in the effort, practicing. That's most students and it's a good mark. An E is for students going well beyond that. An S is not a warning sign.
